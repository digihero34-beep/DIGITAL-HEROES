# DIGITAL HEROES — PREMIUM PRODUCT DESIGN DIRECTIVE
# PART 1 — PRODUCT VISION, UX PHILOSOPHY & VISUAL IDENTITY

IMPORTANT: This is PART 1 of a multi-part directive. Read all parts together as one unified design system. The Digital Heroes PRD is the primary source of truth. Never silently invent or change business rules. When requirements are ambiguous, identify and document the assumption.

## 1. DESIGN MISSION
Do not build a generic website. Build a memorable product experience that communicates product maturity, UX maturity, visual sophistication, trust, emotional intelligence, engineering awareness, and attention to detail.

The goal is not to fake "10 years of experience." Demonstrate the decisions associated with mature product design: restraint, hierarchy, consistency, accessibility, responsive thinking, business understanding, interaction clarity, and system thinking.

The result must feel intentional, premium, modern, human, trustworthy, technically sophisticated, polished, and distinctive.

## 2. PRODUCT-FIRST DESIGN
Digital Heroes combines golf performance tracking, subscriptions, monthly draws, prize pools, charity contribution, charity discovery, winner verification, payout tracking, subscriber dashboards, and administration.

These are one product, not disconnected mini-products.
The narrative should feel like:
PURPOSE → PARTICIPATION → PERFORMANCE → CONTRIBUTION → DRAW → REWARD → VERIFICATION → IMPACT

The interface should make those relationships understandable.

## 3. NO GENERIC TEMPLATE
Do not start from a generic SaaS template or reproduce a common AI-generated layout. Do not automatically use hero + feature cards + testimonials + pricing + FAQ simply because it is familiar.

Every major section must answer:
- Why does this exist?
- What user question does it answer?
- What action or understanding does it support?
- What makes it specifically Digital Heroes?

Remove decoration that has no product purpose.

## 4. PRODUCT POSITIONING
The PRD explicitly says the experience should not resemble a traditional golf website. Golf is a mechanism, not the entire identity.

Avoid making fairways, clubs, plaid, country-club imagery, or conventional golf aesthetics the dominant language.

Prioritize:
- social impact
- participation
- reward
- community
- trust
- transparency
- modern sport culture
- technology

## 5. EMOTIONAL DESIGN
Charity must feel like a core part of the product story, not a footer feature. Users should understand that participation connects to meaningful impact.

Use authentic imagery, impact information, human-centered storytelling, transparent contribution information, and calm emotional hierarchy.

Do not manufacture urgency, fake scarcity, or manipulative emotional pressure.

The tone should be positive, human, purposeful, and trustworthy.

## 6. EMOTION + TRUST
The product must balance excitement with trust, emotion with transparency, premium visuals with usability, and sophistication with simplicity.

The draw may feel exciting, but it must not feel like casino software.
Subscription may feel premium, but must remain transparent.
Charity may feel emotional, but must remain factual.

## 7. VISUAL PERSONALITY
Use a coherent combination of:
Premium editorial design + modern product design + trust-oriented financial UX + social-impact storytelling + advanced sports technology.

Avoid generic SaaS, generic fintech, crypto, casino, gaming, AI-startup, or traditional golf-club visual language.

The visual identity must be ownable.

## 8. RECOGNITION TEST
Periodically remove the logo, company name, and page title.
Ask: "Could this belong to any random SaaS company?"
If yes, strengthen the product-specific identity through composition, typography, storytelling, interaction patterns, data presentation, and charity/reward relationships—not random decoration.

## 9. DESIGN SYSTEM FIRST
Before building many screens, establish:
- typography
- color system
- spacing scale
- layout grid
- containers
- surface hierarchy
- borders
- radius
- shadows
- icon language
- imagery direction
- button/form language
- statuses
- data visualization language
- motion principles

Do not choose each page independently.

## 10. TYPOGRAPHY
Create a hierarchy for Display, H1, H2, H3, H4, Body Large, Body, Body Small, Caption, Metadata, Numerical Display, and Data Labels.

Large type can communicate product purpose, impact, prize values, and major statistics. Do not make everything oversized.

Typography must guide attention and remain readable on desktop, tablet, and mobile.

## 11. NUMERICAL DESIGN
Numbers are important to this product: prize pool, charity contribution, subscribers, scores, winnings, and draw statistics.

Important numbers should feel intentional rather than like ordinary text.

Use numerical hierarchy and subtle animation only when it communicates change or importance. Do not animate every number.

## 12. LAYOUT & GRID
Use a consistent grid with deliberate whitespace, editorial alignment, visual rhythm, and controlled asymmetry.

Asymmetry is allowed when it improves hierarchy, storytelling, attention, or visual interest. Never use asymmetry merely to appear creative.

## 13. INFORMATION HIERARCHY
Every screen must clearly establish:
PRIMARY information → SECONDARY information → SUPPORTING information.

Ask:
- What should I notice first?
- What should I understand next?
- What should I do?
- What happens after I act?

Use typography, scale, spacing, grouping, position, contrast, and restrained motion.

## 14. HOMEPAGE PURPOSE
The homepage must explain:
- what Digital Heroes is
- why it matters
- how participation works
- how charity is involved
- how reward/draws work
- what to do next

The first viewport should communicate product value immediately.

## 15. HERO EXPERIENCE
Avoid a generic welcome banner.
Create a product proposition using strong messaging plus impact/reward signals, meaningful data, and a prominent action.

The hero should feel like the beginning of a product story—not a marketing banner pasted onto a template.

Possible ingredients include editorial typography, meaningful imagery, impact statistics, reward context, subtle motion, and strong whitespace.

Never add random 3D objects, floating cards, blobs, or gradients without a product reason.

## 16. HOMEPAGE NARRATIVE
A useful narrative may be:
Hero → What Digital Heroes is → How participation works → Charity impact → Score experience → Draw experience → Prize transparency → Winner verification → Featured charities → Final CTA.

This is a reasoning framework, not a mandatory template. Change the sequence when a better product argument exists.

## 17. USER JOURNEYS
Design complete journeys.

PUBLIC VISITOR:
Discover → Understand → Explore charity → Understand subscription → Subscribe

SUBSCRIBER:
Login → Dashboard → Enter scores → Manage charity → View participation → View draw → View winnings

WINNER:
Winner status → Proof upload → Review → Verification → Payment status → Paid

ADMIN:
Login → Overview → Users → Subscriptions → Draw configuration → Simulation → Publication → Winners → Payouts → Analytics

Every journey needs a clear goal, primary action, next step, and feedback.

## 18. SUBSCRIPTION EXPERIENCE
Make monthly/yearly plans, price, renewal, cancellation, charity contribution, and participation relationship easy to understand.

Do not hide important commercial information or use dark patterns.

## 19. CHARITY EXPERIENCE
Charity discovery should feel like a primary product capability.

Flow:
Discover → Search → Filter → Explore → Understand → Select → Confirm.

Avoid meaningless card grids. Charity entries should communicate identity, story, impact, events, contribution context, and a useful action.

## 20. CHARITY DETAIL
A charity page should answer:
Who are they?
What do they do?
Why does it matter?
How does the user's contribution connect?
What events or activities exist?
What can the user do next?

Use only supported information. Do not fabricate impact claims.

## 21. SCORE EXPERIENCE
Score entry should feel simple:
Date → Score → Validate → Save → Confirm → Updated five-score set.

Make the rolling five-score behavior understandable. Show current scores, chronological position, and meaningful feedback when a score changes the set.

## 22. DASHBOARD PHILOSOPHY
Do not create a page full of equal cards and charts.
Ask what the user needs to know now.
Prioritize:
1. Current status
2. Important action
3. Upcoming event
4. Progress
5. Impact
6. Rewards
7. History

## 23. TRUST AS A DESIGN REQUIREMENT
The product deals with subscription money, charity, prize pools, winners, and payouts. Trust must therefore be visible in the design.

Users should understand how contribution works, how prize pools work, how draws work, how winner verification works, and where payment stands.

Use breakdowns, process timelines, status indicators, timestamps, and clear explanations.

## 24. VISUAL RESTRAINT
Premium does not mean more gradients, effects, shadows, 3D, animation, or colors.
Premium means better decisions.

Use whitespace, typography, imagery, composition, and purposeful interaction.

## 25. PRODUCT MATURITY
Do not try to make the interface "look experienced."
Demonstrate maturity through clarity, hierarchy, restraint, consistency, accessibility, responsive thinking, and edge-state awareness.

## 26. DESIGN DECISION TEST
For every major design decision ask:
Why is this layout used?
Why is this visual priority correct?
Why does this interaction exist?
Why does this element need motion?
What user problem does it solve?
What product or business purpose does it serve?

If there is no strong answer, simplify.

## 27. EXPERIENCE OVER DECORATION
When decoration conflicts with clarity, choose clarity.
When complex animation conflicts with speed, choose speed.
When more information conflicts with hierarchy, choose hierarchy.

## 28. ORIGINALITY
Originality must come from product reasoning, not random colors, shapes, or effects.

Make the charity/reward relationship, score experience, draw experience, trust system, and status language specific to Digital Heroes.

## 29. REVIEW STANDARD
Review every major screen as a product designer, UX designer, frontend engineer, accessibility reviewer, and hiring manager.

Ask:
Is the primary action obvious?
Is the hierarchy clear?
Does it feel trustworthy?
Does it feel original?
Does it work without explanation?
Does it align with the PRD?
